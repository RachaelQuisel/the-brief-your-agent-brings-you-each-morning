## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you will design a Morning Brief for your own business: the short list an agent brings you before the day starts, naming what it may watch, what it may draft, and the actions it may never take without a named person's approval.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0-8 · The agent that messages you first + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "On October 21, Anthropic opened a waitlist for Claude Duet, an agent that does not wait for you to open a chat. It watches your email and calendar, sends a morning brief on what most needs your attention, drafts replies for your review, and keeps working on its own computer in the cloud when your laptop is closed."
- "Anthropic aimed it at professionals and small business owners. Its own examples: find unpaid invoices and draft the reminders, prepare payroll for review, build briefs, decks, and dashboards."
- "The design choice that matters: it asks for approval before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document, or deletes anything. Four actions wait at a gate, with a person at the gate."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **Checking is the agent's job. Deciding is yours.** The agent watches everything and ranks it. You decide the few things that matter.
2. **Drafting is free. Sending is not.** Let the agent prepare at full speed. Nothing leaves the business without a named person approving it.
3. **The brief must name what it did not touch.** A brief that lists what the agent noticed and left alone, and why, is a brief you can trust and correct.

"Watch everything. Draft freely. Gate the four actions."

### 8-18 · The five boxes of a Morning Brief

Teach the five boxes on the board. Use one shop with nine staff as the running example.

**Box one: the sources.** "Write exactly which accounts the agent may watch. One inbox, one calendar, one accounting feed, one team chat is enough for most businesses. The brief's first line names its sources every day, by name. A brief that cannot name its sources is a rumor, and a source that silently drops out is how a double booking happens."

**Box two: the brief format.** "Five parts, in this order. What changed overnight. What needs a decision today, ranked, no more than three, with the cost of waiting beside each one. Drafts waiting for approval, counted, not dumped on the page. What the agent already handled inside its written permissions, one line each. What it noticed and did not touch, and why. That last part is the trust builder: you can only correct judgment you can see."

**Box three: draft permissions.** "List what the agent may prepare without asking. Replies drafted in your voice, from your own past messages. First invoice reminders, polite, under an amount you set. A payroll summary with any number that changed from last time flagged in plain words. Drafting is cheap and reversible, so this list can be generous."

**Box four: the four actions and the never list.** "Send, pay, sign, delete. Beside each one, write the name of the one person who approves it. Then write the never list: actions the agent may not take at any price, starting with changing a supplier's bank details from an email, the most common way small businesses get robbed. The agent surfaces a never-list item, labels it, and stops."

**Box five: the budget and the clock.** "Write how often the agent checks: at set times you choose, not all day by default. Write a weekly cost limit for the agent's work and review it for the first month like a new hire's timesheet. An agent that works while you sleep also spends while you sleep, and the spending should be a decision, not a surprise."

Then the habit that makes it real:

**The two-week correction rule.** "For the first two weeks, correct the brief every day: what ranked too high, what ranked too low, what should never appear. Those corrections are not setup chores. They are how your judgment gets installed. An owner who skips them gets a generic brief about a generic business."

*Facilitator note:* name the trap out loud. "The failure is not an agent that ranks something wrong in week one. The failure is an owner who stops reading in week three and approves on autopilot. Keep one habit forever: re-read the first draft of the day fully, as a spot check, even when the rest get a quick approval."

### 18-28 · Watch it work

Work one example live. Use a printed inbox summary and a printed brief. Keep it to ten minutes.

1. "Here is the old Tuesday." *(Ninety messages at 6:40 AM. The owner misses a delivery date change, approves a colliding schedule, and finds a three-week-old unpaid invoice only when the customer phones.)* "Nobody here is bad at their job. The information was all present. It was unranked, in four places, at the wrong hour."
2. "Here is the same Tuesday with a brief." *(At 6:30 AM the brief names its sources, lists three decisions with drafts attached, counts seven drafts waiting, reports two items handled inside written permissions, and ends with two items noticed and untouched.)*
3. "Here is the bank details test." *(One untouched item is a request to change a supplier's bank details.)* "The brief does not process it faster. It labels it, states the never-list rule, and waits for a phone call the owner makes herself. That one line is the difference between a summary and a chief of staff."
4. "Here is the approval minute." *(The owner approves two drafts, edits one sentence in the third, and starts the day at the day.)* "Three decisions and one edited sentence. That ratio, many drafts to few decisions, is what every agent launched this week is competing to produce."
5. "Here is the correction." *(The owner moves one item down the ranking and writes why.)* "Thirty seconds. That correction runs every morning from tomorrow on. The brief is the owner's management, written down, running at 6:30 AM."

*Facilitator note:* if the room says they are too small for this, answer with the team-of-one point: "A solo owner has no one to catch the dropped invoice or the unanswered quote request. The brief is the second pair of eyes you could never justify hiring. Hold the four-action gate tighter, not looser, because there is also no one to catch the agent."

### 28-33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you will get, and the answers:

- "What if the agent ranks the wrong thing first?": "It will, in week one. That is what the two-week correction rule is for. Cap the decision list at three and make the agent argue for each place in one sentence. A short list with reasons can be corrected. A long list with no reasons can only be skimmed."
- "Is this just a summary of my email?": "No. A summary tells you what arrived. A brief tells you what needs a decision, brings the drafts, reports what it handled inside its permissions, and names what it refused to touch and why. If a product cannot show you the last part, it is a summary with better marketing."
- "What does it cost me?": "Two costs, both written down in box five. The plan cost, because background checking uses your plan's allowance. And your correction time, about ten minutes a day for two weeks. Budget both on a calm day, before the first busy day makes the decision for you."

### 33-58 · Design your Morning Brief

Everyone works solo or in pairs on their brief design (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "Which four sources may your agent watch? Name them."
- "Read me your three decision slots. What earns a place, and what is the cost of waiting?"
- "Show me your draft permissions. Which first reminder may it send under your written rule, and under what amount?"
- "Read your never list out loud. Is the bank details change on it?"
- "Who approves sending, paying, signing, and deleting? Names, not teams."
- "What are your check times and your weekly cost limit?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their decision list cap and their never list in thirty seconds. "My brief watches ___. My decision list holds ___ items. My never list starts with ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "Four versions, all from real patterns. The brief that cried urgent: twelve items ranked urgent because urgent is safe for the agent and costly for you; fix it with the three-item cap and a one-sentence argument per item. The autopilot approval: week three, coffee, thumb, yes, yes, yes; fix it by fully re-reading the first draft every morning, forever. The quiet source: a connected account drops out and the brief says nothing; fix it with the first line naming sources every day. The helpful payment: an amount with a typo, approved because the form was beautiful; fix it by having amounts above your line read back in words, not digits, so approval slows down at exactly the right second."

### 58-60 · Wrap-up and homework

"Watch everything. Draft freely. Gate the four actions."

Homework: within two weeks, write your one-page Morning Brief design and correct it daily for ten working days if you run a live agent, or run the paper version yourself for three mornings if you do not. Highlight the one ranking rule you changed most often and bring back the page with that rule highlighted and your never list complete. The highlighted rule and the completed never list are the proof the design is real, not a wish.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled worksheet as you say this. "Bring this page back filled in, with your most-changed ranking rule highlighted and your never list complete, keep or fix."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you are tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| Proactive agent | An agent that messages you first, with what needs attention |
| Morning brief | The short ranked list the agent brings you before the day starts |
| Approval gate | The step where a named person must say yes before the action happens |
| Background checks (agent) | The agent looking at your accounts on a schedule you set |
| Usage limits | The amount of agent work your plan includes before it slows or costs more |
| Connector | A link that lets the agent read or work in one of your accounts |
| Skill | Your know-how written down once, in steps, so the agent follows it every time |
| Loop | A job that takes an item, carries out the steps, checks the result, and logs it |
